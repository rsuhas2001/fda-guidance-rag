# DOC_ID: FDA-CCS-1999
# TITLE: FDA Guidance for Industry: Container Closure Systems for Packaging Human Drugs and Biologics
# SOURCE: U.S. Food and Drug Administration (CDER/CBER), May 1999

## Section 1: Introduction and Regulatory Scope
Under Section 501(a)(3) of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, a drug is deemed to be adulterated if its container is composed, in whole or in part, of any poisonous or deleterious substance which may render the contents injurious to health. Furthermore, 21 CFR 211.94 requires that drug product containers and closures shall not be reactive, additive, or absorptive so as to alter the safety, identity, strength, quality, or purity of the drug beyond the official or established requirements.

A container closure system (CCS) refers to the sum of packaging components that together contain and protect the dosage form. This includes primary packaging components (such as glass vials, elastomeric stoppers, prefilled syringe barrels, and plunger stoppers) that come into direct physical contact with the drug product, and secondary packaging components (such as aluminum crimp seals, cartons, and overwraps) that provide additional protection or ensure package integrity.

## Section 2: Qualification Criteria for Container Closure Systems
Every proposed container closure system must be shown to be suitable for its intended use Across four primary dimensions:
1. **Protection**: The CCS must protect the dosage form from factors that cause degradation, including light exposure (photodegradation), reactive gases (such as oxygen), moisture permeation, and microbial contamination.
2. **Compatibility**: The packaging components must not interact physically or chemically with the dosage form in a manner that causes unacceptable changes in the quality of either the drug product or the packaging component (e.g., loss of potency due to adsorption onto elastomeric stoppers, precipitation, or pH shift in unbuffered parenteral solutions).
3. **Safety**: Packaging components must not leach harmful or toxic levels of chemical substances (extractables and leachables) into the drug product under expected storage and administration conditions.
4. **Performance**: The container closure system must function as designed, such as delivering an accurate dose from a prefilled syringe with acceptable break-loose and glide forces, or allowing needle penetration through an elastomeric vial stopper without coring or fragmentation.

## Section 3: Risk-Based Classification by Route of Administration
The FDA establishes a matrix correlating the likelihood of packaging-component interaction with the degree of concern associated with the route of administration:
- **Highest Risk (High Likelihood of Interaction + Highest Degree of Concern)**: Inhalation aerosols and solutions, injectable products (parenterals, sterile powders for injection, prefilled syringes, cartridge systems), and ophthalmic preparations.
- **High Risk**: Transdermal ointments and patches, nasal aerosols and sprays.
- **Moderate to Low Risk**: Topical powders, oral tablets, and hard/soft gelatin capsules.

For sterile injectable products and parenterals, both physicochemical testing and biological reactivity testing (under USP <87> in vitro and USP <88> in vivo) are mandatory for elastomeric closures and plastic components in direct contact with the formulation.

## Section 4: Injectable Vial and Prefilled Syringe Specific Requirements
Injectable drug products require hermetic or sterile barrier integrity throughout shelf life. Elastomeric closures (such as bromobutyl or chlorobutyl rubber stoppers, with or without fluoropolymer film lamination) must be evaluated for:
- **Coring and Fragmentation**: Resistance to shedding elastomer particles when punctured with a hypodermic needle or transfer spike (per FDA CMC elastomeric functionality standards).
- **Self-Sealing Capacity**: Multi-dose parenteral vials must maintain container closure integrity and prevent microbial ingress after multiple needle punctures.
- **Moisture Vapor Permeation**: Lyophilized (freeze-dried) injectable products are highly sensitive to moisture transfer directly from the elastomeric stopper itself during long-term stability storage; stoppers must undergo validated drying cycles to control residual moisture content.
- **Silicone Oil Interaction**: Glass vials and prefilled syringe barrels often use polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) silicone oil lubrication. For biologic proteins and monoclonal antibodies, free silicone oil micro-droplets can induce protein aggregation or sub-visible particulate formation, often necessitating baked-on siliconization or fluoropolymer-coated plunger stoppers.
